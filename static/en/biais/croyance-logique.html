Belief bias

Biais de croyance

Judging the validity of an argument by how plausible its conclusion is, not by its logical structure.

The essentials

Mechanism

Evaluating the form of an argument takes an effort of abstraction. So we substitute an easier question for “does the conclusion follow from the premises?”: “is the conclusion true?”

In practice

Example

“All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. Therefore some roses fade quickly.” Most people judge this argument valid, because its conclusion is plausible. Yet it does not follow from the premises: the flowers that fade quickly could all be tulips.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Separate two questions: are the premises true? Does the conclusion follow from them? Replace the terms with letters (A, B, C) to judge the form.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Fallacy fallacy (fallacy)

Belief bias judges an argument valid or not according to whether its conclusion seems true; the fallacy fallacy judges a conclusion false because the argument defending it is bad.

Going further

Source

Evans, Barston and Pollard (1983). Invalid syllogisms with a believable conclusion are accepted far more often than valid syllogisms with an unbelievable conclusion.
