Cryptomnesia

Inadvertent plagiarism, Source amnesia, Cryptomnésie

Retrieving a memory without recognising it as one, and taking it for a new, personal idea.

The essentials

Mechanism

Memory often keeps the content of a piece of information better than its source. An idea already encountered can thus resurface without the “already seen” label, and seem original.

In practice

Example

In a meeting, an employee enthusiastically puts forward, as his own, an idea that a colleague had presented a week earlier: he has forgotten where he got it.

Famous case

In 1976, Judge Richard Owen concluded that George Harrison’s song “My Sweet Lord” reuses the melody of “He’s So Fine” by the Chiffons (1963). He found that Harrison had not copied deliberately, but unconsciously, a tune he knew.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Note the source of ideas at the moment you come across them. Before claiming an idea, check that it does not already exist.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Misinformation effect

The misinformation effect alters the content of a memory; cryptomnesia erases its origin, so that a memory is taken for a creation.

Going further

Source

The term was proposed by the psychologist Théodore Flournoy around 1900. Brown and Murphy (1989) reproduced the phenomenon in the laboratory: participants put forward as new ideas heard a few minutes earlier.
