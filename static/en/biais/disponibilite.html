Availability heuristic

Availability bias, Heuristique de disponibilité

Estimating how frequent or probable an event is by how easily examples of it come to mind.

The essentials

Mechanism

Ease of recall is a convenient cue, often correlated with real frequency. But it also depends on factors unrelated to frequency: how spectacular an event is, how recent, how widely covered in the media, how emotionally charged.

In practice

Example

After a plane crash makes the news, many people overestimate the risk of flying, even though, per distance travelled, the risk of a fatal accident is far higher by car.

Famous case

After the attacks of 11 September 2001, many Americans swapped flying for driving. Gerd Gigerenzer (2006) estimates that this caused about 1,600 additional road deaths in the following twelve months. The explanation is disputed: Su et al. (2009) find the rise mainly in the Northeast, near the attack sites, and attribute it to stress rather than to people switching from planes to cars.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Separate striking examples from data. Ask what the event’s base frequency is, and why some cases are easier to recall than others.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Illusory truth effect

Availability concerns the frequency we attribute to an event; the illusory truth effect concerns the credibility we give to a repeated claim.

Going further

Source

Tversky and Kahneman (1973). Participants judged words beginning with certain consonants (K, R) to be more frequent than words with those letters in third position, when the opposite is true: the former are simply easier to search for in memory.
