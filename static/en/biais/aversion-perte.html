Loss aversion

Aversion à la perte

A loss has a stronger psychological impact than a gain of the same size.

The essentials

Mechanism

According to prospect theory, the value of an outcome depends on a reference point, and the curve is steeper on the loss side: a loss weighs about twice as much as an equivalent gain.

In practice

Example

Most people turn down a coin-toss bet in which they win €100 or lose €100, although its expected value is zero. A gain of around €200 often has to be offered for them to accept.

Famous case

Pope and Schweitzer (2011), on more than 2.5 million putts on the US professional tour (PGA): at equal distance, golfers sink about 2 percentage points fewer of their putts for birdie (gaining a stroke) than of their putts for par (avoiding losing a stroke).

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Evaluate each option by its end result, not relative to a reference point. Ask yourself: if I had nothing today, would I choose to buy this position?

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Endowment effect

Loss aversion is the general principle; the endowment effect is one consequence of it, applied to objects we own.

Going further

Source

Kahneman and Tversky (1979), prospect theory. Tversky and Kahneman (1992) estimate the loss aversion coefficient at about 2.25.
