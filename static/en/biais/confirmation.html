Confirmation bias

Biais de confirmation

Preferentially seeking, interpreting and remembering information that confirms what one already believes.

The essentials

Mechanism

Testing a hypothesis by looking for cases that confirm it takes less effort than looking for what would refute it. Contrary information is also more unpleasant, and so examined more severely.

In practice

Example

A person convinced of a remedy’s effectiveness remembers the times they got better after taking it, and forgets the times when nothing happened.

Famous case

The Brandon Mayfield case (2004). After the Madrid bombings, the FBI matched a fingerprint to an American lawyer. Three experts confirmed the identification, until the Spanish police identified the real culprit. The report of the Justice Department’s Inspector General (2006) noted that the examiners had been asked to confirm a colleague’s identification rather than examine it independently, and that ambiguous details were interpreted in favour of that identification. The Spanish police’s warnings were not enough to call it into question.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Actively look for what would refute your hypothesis. Ask what observation would make you change your mind, and whether you have really looked for it.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Belief bias

Confirmation bias concerns how information is sought and interpreted; belief bias, how the validity of a line of reasoning is assessed.

Going further

Source

Peter Wason (1960), the “2-4-6” task. Participants must guess the rule (in fact: “three increasing numbers”). They propose almost only sequences that fit their hypothesis, instead of trying to refute it.
