Simpson’s paradox

Yule-Simpson effect, Paradoxe de Simpson

A trend that appears in each of several groups disappears or reverses when the data are combined.

The essentials

Mechanism

The groups are not the same size, and a hidden variable influences both group membership and the outcome. Intuition wrongly assumes that an overall average reflects the averages of each group.

In practice

Example

A treatment can be better for mild patients and for severe patients, yet look worse overall, if it is mostly given to severe patients.

Famous case

University of California, Berkeley, 1973: 44% of male applicants to graduate programmes are admitted, against 35% of female applicants. Bickel, Hammel and O’Connell (1975) show that, department by department, women are not disadvantaged: they applied more to the most selective departments.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Before drawing a conclusion from an overall average, break it down by subgroup. Look for variables that could influence both the distribution and the outcome.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Correlation mistaken for causation (fallacy)

Correlation mistaken for causation overlooks that a common factor can create an apparent link; Simpson’s paradox shows that such a factor can even reverse the direction of a comparison.

Going further

Source

Edward Simpson (1951). The phenomenon had been described before him by Karl Pearson (1899) and George Udny Yule (1903).
