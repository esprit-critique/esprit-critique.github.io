The 51 cognitive biases

A cognitive bias is a systematic error of judgement: it happens to almost everyone, in the same direction. Each entry explains where it comes from and how to protect yourself.

Probabilities

Estimating chances, frequencies and risks.

  • Availability heuristic : Estimating how frequent or probable an event is by how easily examples of it come to mind.
  • Base rate neglect : Ignoring how common a phenomenon is to begin with (its prior probability) in favour of a more striking piece of specific information.
  • Conjunction fallacy : Judging that a combination of two events is more probable than one of them alone, which is mathematically impossible.
  • Gambler’s fallacy : Believing that an independent random event becomes more or less probable depending on previous results.
  • Regression fallacy : Attributing a specific cause to a return towards normal that is simply explained by statistical variability.
  • Survivorship bias : Drawing conclusions from a sample that contains only the cases that passed a selection, forgetting those that were eliminated.
  • Prosecutor’s fallacy : Confusing the probability of observing a piece of evidence if the accused is innocent with the probability that the accused is innocent given that evidence.
  • Simpson’s paradox : A trend that appears in each of several groups disappears or reverses when the data are combined.
  • Insensitivity to sample size : Giving as much credit to a result from a small sample as to one from a large sample, although small samples vary far more.

Decision

Choosing between options, weighing gains and losses.

  • Anchoring effect : A numerical estimate remains influenced by a value presented earlier, even when that value is arbitrary.
  • Framing effect : The same information leads to different choices depending on how it is presented, for example in terms of gains or losses.
  • Loss aversion : A loss has a stronger psychological impact than a gain of the same size.
  • Sunk cost fallacy : Persisting with a project or a choice because of the resources already invested, when only future costs and benefits should count.
  • Status quo bias : Preferring the current situation or the default option, even when an alternative would be more advantageous.
  • Endowment effect : Attaching more value to an object simply because you own it.
  • Decoy effect : Adding an option that is clearly inferior to one of the existing options makes that option more attractive.
  • IKEA effect : Attaching more value to an object that you have made or assembled yourself, even partially.
  • Outcome bias : Judging the quality of a decision by its outcome, rather than by the information and reasoning available when it was made.
  • Omission bias : Judging a harmful action as worse than an equally harmful inaction, and preferring to do nothing when acting carries a risk.
  • Impact bias : Overestimating the intensity and duration of the emotions a future event will cause, whether happy or unhappy.

Memory and attention

What we notice, and what we remember.

  • Hindsight bias : Once the outcome is known, judging that it was predictable, and believing that you had anticipated it yourself.
  • Misinformation effect : Information received after an event alters the memory we keep of it.
  • Primacy effect : The first pieces of information received carry a disproportionate weight in the final impression.
  • Negativity bias : Giving more attention and weight to negative information, experiences and events than to positive ones of equal intensity.
  • Peak-end rule : Judging a past experience mainly by its most intense moment and its end, with very little regard for its duration.
  • Frequency illusion : Having noticed something for the first time, feeling that you meet it everywhere, and concluding that it has become more frequent.
  • Cryptomnesia : Retrieving a memory without recognising it as one, and taking it for a new, personal idea.

Beliefs

Forming, testing and defending convictions.

  • Confirmation bias : Preferentially seeking, interpreting and remembering information that confirms what one already believes.
  • Belief bias : Judging the validity of an argument by how plausible its conclusion is, not by its logical structure.
  • Illusory truth effect : A repeated claim seems truer, regardless of its accuracy.
  • Barnum effect : Accepting as an accurate description of oneself a portrait vague enough, and general enough, to apply to almost everyone.
  • Illusion of control : Overestimating the influence of one’s own actions on events that actually depend on chance.
  • Apophenia : Perceiving meaningful patterns or connections in random or unrelated data.
  • Motivated reasoning : Evaluating arguments and data so as to reach the conclusion one wants, rather than the one they support.
  • Belief perseverance : Continuing to hold a belief as true after the information on which it was based has been discredited.
  • Just-world hypothesis : Believing that everyone gets what they deserve, and concluding that victims are partly responsible for their fate.

Social

Judging others and placing ourselves in a group.

  • Fundamental attribution error : Explaining other people’s behaviour by their personality rather than by the situation, underestimating the weight of circumstances.
  • Halo effect : One perceived characteristic of a person influences the judgement made about their other characteristics, with no logical link between them.
  • False consensus effect : Overestimating the proportion of people who share our opinions, tastes or behaviours.
  • Conformity : Aligning one’s judgements or behaviour with those of a group, even against one’s own perception.
  • Groupthink : In a close-knit group, the search for consensus takes precedence over critical examination of the options, leading to poor decisions.
  • Obedience to authority : Carrying out the orders of an authority perceived as legitimate, even when they contradict one’s own values.
  • Mere exposure effect : Repeated exposure to a stimulus is enough to increase the preference we give it.
  • Curse of knowledge : Once you know something, it becomes hard to picture the point of view of someone who does not.
  • Ingroup bias : Favouring the members of one’s own group, in judgements as well as in actions, even when that group was formed on an arbitrary criterion.

Self-image

Assessing our own abilities and prospects.

  • Dunning-Kruger effect : People who are least competent in a field tend to greatly overestimate their level.
  • Planning fallacy : Underestimating the time, cost and risks of a future task, even when similar tasks have already overrun their forecasts.
  • Self-serving bias : Attributing one’s successes to personal qualities and one’s failures to outside factors.
  • Optimism bias : Believing oneself less exposed than average to negative events, and more likely than average to experience positive ones.
  • Bias blind spot : Readily recognising cognitive biases in others while believing oneself less influenced.
  • Overprecision : Being too certain of the accuracy of one’s knowledge or estimates: the margins of error one allows are too narrow.
