Overprecision

Overconfidence in one’s estimates, Excès de précision

Being too certain of the accuracy of one’s knowledge or estimates: the margins of error one allows are too narrow.

The essentials

Mechanism

We build an estimate from what comes to mind, without accounting for all that we do not know. The sources of error we do not think of are not built into the margin.

In practice

Example

An engineer announces that a prototype will be delivered “between 10 and 15 March”. It arrives on 2 May.

Famous case

In Alpert and Raiffa’s study, students had to give 98% confidence intervals: the true value fell outside in 42% of cases, instead of 2%. For 90% intervals, studies typically find that the true value is included in only 30 to 60% of cases (McKenzie, Liersch and Yaniv, 2008).

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Deliberately widen your ranges. Estimate the lower bound (“I’m almost certain it’s at least…”) and the upper bound separately. Write down your forecasts to measure your own calibration.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Dunning-Kruger effect

Overprecision concerns the width of margins of error, in beginners and experts alike; the Dunning-Kruger effect, the overestimation of their level by the least competent.

Going further

Source

Alpert and Raiffa (1969 report, published in 1982). Moore and Healy (2008) distinguish overprecision from overestimation of one’s abilities and the “better than average” effect.
