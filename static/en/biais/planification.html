Planning fallacy

Erreur de planification

Underestimating the time, cost and risks of a future task, even when similar tasks have already overrun their forecasts.

The essentials

Mechanism

We plan from the ideal scenario of the particular project (the “inside view”), neglecting the statistics of comparable projects (the “outside view”). Unforeseen events, numerous but individually unlikely, are not counted.

In practice

Example

Major public works very often exceed their budget and deadlines. The Sydney Opera House opened in 1973 about ten years late, at 102 million Australian dollars instead of the 7 million estimated, roughly fourteen times more.

Famous case

Flyvbjerg, Holm and Buhl (2002) study 258 major transport infrastructure projects: costs are underestimated in nearly 9 cases out of 10, with an average overrun of 28%. Berlin Brandenburg Airport, planned for 2011, opened in 2020.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Start from the actual durations of comparable projects (“reference class forecasting”), then adjust. Add an explicit margin.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Optimism bias

The planning fallacy is a form of optimism applied to the time and cost of our own projects; it persists even when we know that previous projects ran late. Optimism bias concerns, more broadly, the odds of any event, good or bad.

Going further

Source

Kahneman and Tversky (1979). Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994): students estimate on average that they will finish their dissertation in 34 days; it takes them 56 on average.
