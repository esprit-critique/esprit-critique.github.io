Optimism bias

Unrealistic optimism, Biais d'optimisme

Believing oneself less exposed than average to negative events, and more likely than average to experience positive ones.

The essentials

Mechanism

We spontaneously think of the reasons that protect us (prudence, competence), but not of those that protect others too. This bias reduces anxiety, but leads us to neglect precautions.

In practice

Example

A woman starting a business knows that plenty of new businesses fail, but judges that hers runs almost no risk, without being able to say how it differs from the others.

Famous case

Ola Svenson (1981): 93% of a sample of American students and 69% of a Swedish sample consider themselves more skilful drivers than the median driver. By definition, only half can be. The result was replicated by Koppel et al. (Meta-Psychology, 2023) in a preregistered study of 1,203 participants. This result illustrates the “better-than-average” effect, a neighbour of optimism bias: what people overrate here is their abilities, not their chances of future events.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Reason from the statistics of the general population, then justify any departure in your own favour with specific facts.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Overprecision

Optimism bias skews how we rate our chances of future events, good or bad; overprecision overstates the accuracy of our own estimates.

Going further

Source

Neil Weinstein (1980). Students rate their own chances of experiencing various negative events as lower than their classmates’, and their chances of positive events as higher.
