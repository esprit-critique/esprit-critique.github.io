Ingroup bias

Ingroup favouritism, Favoritisme envers son groupe

Favouring the members of one’s own group, in judgements as well as in actions, even when that group was formed on an arbitrary criterion.

The essentials

Mechanism

According to social identity theory (Tajfel and Turner), belonging to a group feeds self-esteem: valuing “our own” amounts to valuing ourselves. Mere categorisation, even a meaningless one, is enough to produce the effect.

In practice

Example

In a recruitment process, a panel judges candidates from the same school as its members more favourably.

Famous case

In April 1968, the day after the assassination of Martin Luther King, the schoolteacher Jane Elliott divides her class in Riceville, Iowa, by eye colour and declares one group superior. Within a day, disparagement and favouritism appear among the pupils. It was a classroom demonstration, not a controlled experiment.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Evaluate blind where possible. Ask whether you would judge a person from another group the same way if they had done exactly the same thing.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: False consensus effect

The false consensus effect is believing that others think as we do; ingroup bias is judging more favourably those who belong to the same group as us.

Going further

Source

Henri Tajfel, Michael Billig, Robert Bundy and Claude Flament (1971), the minimal group paradigm: teenagers in Bristol, split into two groups according to their supposed preference for Klee or Kandinsky, award more rewards to members of their own group, to the point of sacrificing part of the total gain to widen the gap with the other group.
