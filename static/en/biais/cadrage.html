Framing effect

Effet de cadrage

The same information leads to different choices depending on how it is presented, for example in terms of gains or losses.

The essentials

Mechanism

Options are not evaluated in absolute terms but relative to a reference point suggested by the wording. Faced with gains we prefer certainty; faced with losses we are more willing to take risks.

In practice

Example

A yoghurt that is “95% fat-free” seems healthier than a yoghurt “containing 5% fat”.

Famous case

McNeil, Pauker, Sox and Tversky (1982): for lung cancer, the choice between surgery and radiotherapy changes depending on whether outcomes are presented as survival rates or mortality rates. The effect shows up in patients, but also in doctors.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Restate the information in the opposite frame (gains ↔ losses, success ↔ failure) and check that your preference does not change.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Anchoring effect

Framing comes from the wording of identical information; anchoring, from a starting number that steers an estimate.

Going further

Source

Tversky and Kahneman (1981), the “Asian disease” problem (600 people at risk). 72% choose the certain option when it is worded in terms of lives saved. 78% choose the risky option when the same choice is worded in terms of deaths.
