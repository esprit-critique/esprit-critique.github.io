Gambler’s fallacy

Monte Carlo fallacy, Erreur du joueur

Believing that an independent random event becomes more or less probable depending on previous results.

The essentials

Mechanism

We expect a short sequence to already resemble the overall distribution (the “law of small numbers”). Yet an independent draw has no memory: the law of large numbers works not by compensation but by dilution.

In practice

Example

After five heads in a row, many people think tails is now more likely. The probability remains 1/2.

Famous case

Monte Carlo Casino, 18 August 1913: black comes up 26 times in a row at roulette. Gamblers lose considerable sums betting on red, persuaded that it “has to” come up.

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Check whether the events are really independent. If they are, the history tells you nothing about the next draw.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Illusion of control

The gambler’s fallacy concerns the odds of a draw (“red has to come up”); the illusion of control concerns the ability to influence that draw (“if I throw the dice myself…”).

Going further

Source

Tversky and Kahneman (1971), “Belief in the law of small numbers”.
