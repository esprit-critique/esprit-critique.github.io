Outcome bias

Biais du résultat

Judging the quality of a decision by its outcome, rather than by the information and reasoning available when it was made.

The essentials

Mechanism

The outcome is visible and striking; the decision process and the role of chance are not. A good decision can turn out badly through bad luck, and a bad one can succeed by luck.

In practice

Example

A driver who gets home drunk without an accident concludes that driving after drinking was “not that risky”.

Famous case

Super Bowl, February 2015: with 26 seconds left, Seattle attempts a pass from the one-yard line, which is intercepted by Malcolm Butler. Coach Pete Carroll is accused of making one of the worst decisions in American football history; several analysts nevertheless defended the choice. Poker player Annie Duke used it as the opening example of her book “Thinking in Bets” (2018).

Defending yourself

How to protect yourself

Separate the decision from its outcome: what should have been decided, given the information available at the time? Judge decisions over many cases, not just one.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Hindsight bias

Hindsight bias makes us believe the outcome was predictable; outcome bias makes us judge the decision good or bad according to that outcome. The two often go together.

Going further

Source

Jonathan Baron and John Hershey (1988): participants rate the same medical decision as better when told it turned out well than when told it turned out badly.
