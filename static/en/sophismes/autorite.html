Appeal to authority

Argumentum ad verecundiam, Argument d'autorité

Holding a claim to be true because a person presented as an authority supports it, outside their field of competence or with no other justification.

The essentials

Argument diagram

X says P.

X is an authority.

So P is true.

Why the argument is invalid

Relying on experts is often reasonable, provided we treat them as a source of probability, not of certainty. The reasoning becomes fallacious when the authority is not competent in the field, when it opposes the consensus of its peers, or when it is presented as proof that cannot be discussed.

In practice

Example

“A Nobel Prize winner in physics says this vaccine is dangerous, so it is.”

Famous case

Linus Pauling, twice a Nobel laureate (chemistry in 1954, peace in 1962), promoted from the 1970s large doses of vitamin C against the common cold, then against cancer. Clinical trials did not confirm these effects, but his prestige lastingly fixed the idea in the public mind.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask whether the person is competent in that precise field, whether they express the consensus of their discipline, and what evidence they rely on.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Obedience to authority (bias)

The appeal to authority consists of believing a claim because an authority supports it; obedience to authority, of following its orders.

Going further

Source

Latin name proposed by John Locke (An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, 1690, Book IV, Chapter 17).
