fallacy · Causation

Correlation mistaken for causation

Cum hoc ergo propter hoc, Corrélation prise pour causalité

Concluding that two phenomena are linked by cause and effect because they vary together.

The essentials

Argument diagram

A and B vary together.

So A causes B.

Why the argument is invalid

A correlation can have other explanations: a common cause (a confounder), reverse causation (B causes A), or coincidence, very frequent when many variables are compared.

In practice

Example

Ice cream sales and drownings rise together. The common cause is the heat, which also increases swimming.

Famous case

Hormone therapy for the menopause: observational studies showed that treated women had less heart disease. In 2002, the Women’s Health Initiative randomised trial found instead a slight increase in risk. In the observational studies, the women taking the treatment were, on average, better off and in better health.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Look for a common factor, consider reverse causation, and ask whether there is a controlled study (a randomised trial).

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Post hoc ergo propter hoc

Mistaking correlation for causation relies on joint variation; post hoc, on mere succession in time.

Going further

Source

Tyler Vigen, “Spurious Correlations” (2015): for example, the number of films starring Nicolas Cage and the number of swimming-pool drownings in the United States.
