Begging the question

Circular reasoning, Petitio principii, Pétition de principe

Using as a premise, explicitly or in another form, the conclusion one is trying to prove.

The essentials

Argument diagram

P, because Q.

Q, because P.

Why the argument is invalid

The argument is formally valid, but proves nothing: it convinces only those who already accept the conclusion. The circularity is often hidden by rewording.

In practice

Example

“This book tells the truth, because it says so itself, and a book that tells the truth cannot lie.”

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask for a justification independent of the conclusion: “How do we know that without first assuming it is true?”

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Loaded question

Begging the question assumes the conclusion in the premises of an argument; the loaded question slips a presupposition into a question.

Going further

Source

Described by Aristotle in the Prior Analytics (Book II, chapter 16). “Petitio principii” literally means “asking for the starting point to be granted”.
