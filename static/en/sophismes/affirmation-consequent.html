fallacy · Logic and proof

Affirming the consequent

Affirmation du conséquent

Concluding that the condition of an implication is true because its consequence is.

The essentials

Argument diagram

If P then Q.

Q.

So P.

Why the argument is invalid

P ⇒ Q does not imply Q ⇒ P (the converse). Q can be true for other reasons. The valid reasoning is modus ponens: P ⇒ Q, P, therefore Q.

In practice

Example

“If it rained, the road is wet. The road is wet. So it rained.” It may have been watered.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Look for other possible causes of Q. Check that you are not using the converse of the implication.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Denying the antecedent

Both invert an implication “if P then Q”: affirming the consequent concludes from Q to P, denying the antecedent concludes from not-P to not-Q.

Going further

Source

A formal fallacy known since ancient logic. Aristotle mentions it in the Sophistical Refutations (chapter 5): since the ground is wet after rain, we suppose that it has rained when the ground is wet.
