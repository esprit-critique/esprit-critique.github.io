Esprit critique

The 39 fallacies

A fallacy is reasoning that looks valid but isn’t. Each entry shows the form of the argument, why it fails and how to respond.

Personal attack

Going after the person rather than the argument.

  • Personal attack : Rejecting an argument by attacking the person who makes it (their character, interests or past) rather than the argument itself.
  • You too : Dismissing a criticism by pointing out that the person making it does not follow what they advocate themselves.
  • Guilt by association : Discrediting an idea because it is also defended by a person or group considered despicable.

Illegitimate appeal

Invoking what proves nothing: numbers, age, emotion, threats.

  • Appeal to authority : Holding a claim to be true because a person presented as an authority supports it, outside their field of competence or with no other justification.
  • Appeal to popularity : Considering a claim true or an action good because a large number of people go along with it.
  • Appeal to tradition : Justifying a practice or an idea by its age.
  • Appeal to nature : Considering something good because it is “natural”, or bad because it is “artificial”.
  • Appeal to emotion : Trying to make a conclusion accepted by stirring an emotion (fear, pity, anger, guilt) instead of providing reasons.
  • Appeal to consequences : Judging a claim true or false according to whether its consequences are desirable or not.
  • Genetic fallacy : Judging an idea true or false, good or bad, by its origin rather than its merits.
  • Appeal to force : Trying to make a conclusion accepted through a threat, explicit or veiled, rather than through reasons.

Evasion

Dodging the question, distorting or shifting the debate.

  • Straw man : Distorting an opponent’s position, by caricaturing or exaggerating it, in order to refute it more easily.
  • Red herring : Introducing a subject unrelated to the question in order to divert attention from the point under debate.
  • Loaded question : Asking a question that contains an unestablished presupposition, so that any direct answer seems to validate it.
  • Moving the goalposts : When the evidence asked for is provided, demanding different or more demanding evidence, without acknowledging that the first request has been met.
  • Gish gallop : Overwhelming the other party with a large number of weak or false arguments, faster than it is possible to answer them.

False comparison

Wrongly reducing, equating or bringing options closer together.

  • False dilemma : Presenting two options as the only possible ones, when others exist.
  • Middle ground fallacy : Assuming that the truth necessarily lies halfway between two opposing positions.
  • False analogy : Concluding that one thing has a property because it resembles another that has it, when the resemblance does not concern what matters.
  • False equivalence : Presenting two things as equivalent because they share a trait, when they differ greatly in seriousness, scale or soundness.
  • Nirvana fallacy : Rejecting a realistic solution because it is not perfect, by comparing it with an ideal solution that does not exist.

Causation

Jumping to a cause-and-effect conclusion.

  • Post hoc ergo propter hoc : Concluding that one event caused another because it came before it.
  • Correlation mistaken for causation : Concluding that two phenomena are linked by cause and effect because they vary together.
  • Slippery slope : Claiming that a first measure will inevitably lead to a series of ever more serious consequences, without justifying each step.
  • Texas sharpshooter fallacy : Spotting a cluster in random data after the fact and presenting it as significant, while ignoring the data that do not fit it.
  • Single cause fallacy : Attributing a complex phenomenon to a single cause, when it results from several factors combined.

Generalisation

Wrongly going from a few cases to a rule.

  • Hasty generalisation : Drawing a general conclusion from too few cases, or from unrepresentative ones.
  • No true Scotsman : Faced with a counterexample, changing the definition of a group after the fact to exclude it and protect a generalisation.
  • Composition and division : Attributing to a whole the properties of its parts (composition), or to the parts the properties of the whole (division).
  • Cherry-picking : Presenting only the data that support a conclusion, passing over those, sometimes the majority, that contradict it.

Logic and proof

Errors of structure and of burden of proof.

  • Affirming the consequent : Concluding that the condition of an implication is true because its consequence is.
  • Denying the antecedent : Concluding that the consequence of an implication is false because its condition is.
  • Begging the question : Using as a premise, explicitly or in another form, the conclusion one is trying to prove.
  • Appeal to ignorance : Concluding that a claim is true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.
  • Fallacy fallacy : Concluding that a claim is false because the argument presented in its defence is fallacious.

Language

Playing on the meaning of words.

  • Equivocation : Using the same word in two different senses within an argument, which makes it invalid.
  • Quoting out of context : Quoting a sentence cut off from its context, so that it seems to say something else, even the opposite, of what its author meant.
  • Continuum fallacy : Concluding that a distinction does not exist, or does not matter, because one passes from one to the other by imperceptible steps, with no sharp boundary.
  • Persuasive definition : Redefining a value-laden word in order to apply it, or refuse it, to what one wants to praise or condemn, while keeping its emotional charge.
