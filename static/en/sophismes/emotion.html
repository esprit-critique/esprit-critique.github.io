Appeal to emotion

Argumentum ad passiones, Appeal to fear, Appeal to pity, Appel à l'émotion

Trying to make a conclusion accepted by stirring an emotion (fear, pity, anger, guilt) instead of providing reasons.

The essentials

Argument diagram

Thinking not-P provokes a strong emotion.

So P.

Why the argument is invalid

An emotion can be legitimate and relevant, but it is not proof. The appeal to emotion becomes fallacious when it replaces argument or prevents the facts from being examined.

In practice

Example

“If you don’t buy this alarm, imagine what could happen to your children.”

Famous case

“Daisy” (1964), a Lyndon Johnson campaign ad: a little girl pulls the petals off a daisy, then a countdown ends in a nuclear explosion. The ad contains no argument about his opponent’s programme; it merely suggests danger.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Acknowledge the emotion, then return to the question: “I understand, but what are the reasons to think it is true?”

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Appeal to consequences

The appeal to emotion stirs a feeling in place of reasons; the appeal to consequences judges a claim true or false according to whether its effects would be desirable.

Going further

Source

In the Rhetoric (Book I, chapter 2), Aristotle distinguishes pathos (emotion), ethos (the speaker’s credibility) and logos (reasoning).
