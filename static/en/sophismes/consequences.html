Appeal to consequences

Argumentum ad consequentiam, Appel aux conséquences

Judging a claim true or false according to whether its consequences are desirable or not.

The essentials

Argument diagram

If P were true, it would be terrible.

So P is false.

Why the argument is invalid

Reality does not adjust to our preferences. The consequences of a belief may be relevant to deciding what to do, but not to establishing what is true.

In practice

Example

“Climate change can’t be that serious: it would mean we have to transform our whole economy.”

Defending yourself

How to respond

Separate two questions: “Is it true?” and “What should we do if it is?” Answering the second does not settle the first.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Slippery slope

The slippery slope predicts a chain of consequences in order to reject a measure; the appeal to consequences rejects a claim because its consequences would be unpleasant, without discussing the evidence.

Going further

Source

David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature (1739, Book II, Part 3, Section 2): refuting a hypothesis by pointing to its dangerous consequences for religion and morality is, in his words, the most common and the most blameable method of reasoning.
