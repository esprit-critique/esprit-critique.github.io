S 22Fallacy number 22,
fallacy · Causation

Post hoc ergo propter hoc

“After this, therefore because of this”

Concluding that one event caused another because it came before it.

The essentials

Argument diagram

A happened, then B.

So A caused B.

Why the argument is invalid

Succession in time is necessary for causation, but not sufficient. B may have other causes, be a coincidence, or result from natural change (many illnesses clear up on their own).

In practice

Example

“I wore my lucky jumper and we won. It’s thanks to the jumper.”

Famous case

In 1998, Andrew Wakefield published in The Lancet a study of 12 children suggesting a link between the MMR vaccine and autism, relying notably on symptoms appearing after vaccination. But the first signs of autism often appear at the age when this vaccine is given. The paper was retracted in 2010, and large studies have found no link.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask what would have happened without A. Only a comparison with a control group allows you to conclude there is an effect.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Correlation mistaken for causation

Post hoc relies on succession in time (A then B); mistaking correlation for causation, on joint variation (A and B together).

Going further

Source

A Latin formula from scholastic logic.
