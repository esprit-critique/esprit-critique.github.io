fallacy · Generalisation

Hasty generalisation

Secundum quid, Généralisation hâtive

Drawing a general conclusion from too few cases, or from unrepresentative ones.

The essentials

Argument diagram

A few observed Xs have property P.

So all Xs have P.

Why the argument is invalid

A small sample is very sensitive to chance, and a non-random sample can be biased. Personal anecdotes are particularly misleading, because they are striking.

In practice

Example

“My grandfather smoked all his life and lived to be 95, so tobacco isn’t that dangerous.”

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask how many cases were observed, how they were chosen, and what broader data say.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Cherry-picking

Hasty generalisation concludes from too few cases; cherry-picking, from cases chosen from among others that contradict the conclusion.

Going further

Source

John Stuart Mill, A System of Logic (1843), Book V, chapter 5, “Fallacies of Generalization”: following Bacon, he criticises induction by simple enumeration, which rests on fewer instances than it should.
