Slippery slope

Pente glissante

Claiming that a first measure will inevitably lead to a series of ever more serious consequences, without justifying each step.

The essentials

Argument diagram

If A, then B; if B, then C; …; so Z (catastrophic).

So not A.

Why the argument is invalid

Each step is only possible, and the probabilities multiply: a chain of ten steps, each 80% likely, has only about an 11% chance of happening in full. The reasoning is fallacious when the links are not demonstrated.

In practice

Example

“If we allow phones at break time, they’ll soon be in class, and then nobody will listen to lessons any more.”

Famous case

The “domino theory”, set out by President Eisenhower on 7 April 1954: if Indochina fell under communist control, the whole of Southeast Asia would follow. It was used to justify American involvement in Vietnam.

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask for each link in the chain to be justified: “Why would B necessarily follow from A?”

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Appeal to consequences

The slippery slope announces a chain of undemonstrated steps; the appeal to consequences rejects a claim because of its consequences, with no intermediate chain.

Going further

Source

Douglas Walton, “Slippery Slope Arguments” (1992): not all arguments of this type are fallacious; they are legitimate when each step is supported.
