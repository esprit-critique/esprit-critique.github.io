Appeal to ignorance

Argumentum ad ignorantiam, Burden-of-proof reversal, Appel à l'ignorance

Concluding that a claim is true because it has not been proven false, or false because it has not been proven true.

The essentials

Argument diagram

It has not been proven that P is false.

So P is true.

Why the argument is invalid

The absence of evidence against a claim is not evidence in its favour. The burden of proof lies with whoever makes the claim: otherwise, any unverifiable claim would have to be accepted.

In practice

Example

“Nobody has ever proved that ghosts don’t exist, so they do.”

Famous case

Joseph McCarthy (1950), about a person on his list of alleged communists: “I do not have much information on this except the general statement of the agency that there is nothing in the files to disprove his Communist connections.”

Defending yourself

How to respond

Remind the person that the burden of proof lies with whoever makes the claim. Bertrand Russell: we cannot prove that no teapot is orbiting the Sun, but that is no reason to believe in one.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Moving the goalposts

The appeal to ignorance holds a claim true for lack of contrary evidence; moving the goalposts rejects the evidence provided by changing the requirement.

Going further

Source

Latin name proposed by John Locke (An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, 1690, Book IV, Chapter 17), for whom the argument consists in requiring the opponent to accept what is put forward as proof or to provide a better one. Bertrand Russell, “celestial teapot” (1952).
