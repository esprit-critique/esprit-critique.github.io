Appeal to nature

Argumentum ad naturam, Appel à la nature

Considering something good because it is “natural”, or bad because it is “artificial”.

The essentials

Argument diagram

X is natural.

So X is good (or healthy, or desirable).

Why the argument is invalid

“Natural” implies neither “good” nor “healthy”. Arsenic, hemlock and earthquakes are natural; glasses and antibiotics are not. This moves from a fact (“that’s how it is”) to a norm (“that’s how it ought to be”).

In practice

Example

“This remedy is plant-based, so it can’t have side effects.”

Defending yourself

How to respond

Ask for evidence of the effect claimed, regardless of the origin of the product.

Don’t confuse

≠ Not to be confused with: Appeal to tradition

The appeal to nature invokes the natural character of something; the appeal to tradition, the age of a practice.

Going further

Source

Linked to David Hume’s distinction between is and ought (1739). Distinct from the “naturalistic fallacy” of G. E. Moore (1903), which concerns the definition of the good.
